Historical Mysteries & Open Questions

Kailasa Temple is often clouded by sensational pseudo-archaeology. Yet the true scholarly debates—regarding top-down subtractive quarry logistics, multi-reign construction phases, missing basalt tailings, and sacred Himalayan symbolism—are vastly more profound than any manufactured myth.

Established Evidence Scholarly Interpretation

The Krishna I Attribution & Multi-Phase Patronage

The Evidence: The temple contains no foundation inscription. Its attribution to Rashtrakuta King Krishna I (r. c. 756–774 CE) rests primarily on the Baroda copper plate grant of Karka II (dated 812 CE), which poetically states that Krishna I caused a wondrous Shiva temple at Elapura to be constructed, eliciting awe even from the celestial gods.

The Debate: Prominent archaeologists including M.K. Dhavalikar, Walter Spink, and H. Goetz argue that an excavation of this magnitude (~400,000 tonnes of basalt) likely spanned multiple monarchs: commenced under Dantidurga (who inaugurated Rashtrakuta monuments at Ellora), executed primarily under Krishna I, and embellished across subsequent reigns including Govinda II and Amoghavarsha I.

Established Evidence Scholarly Interpretation

Top-Down Subtractive Engineering

The Method: Unlike structural temples built by stacking quarried blocks, Kailasa was excavated subtractively from the basalt mountain peak down to the courtyard floor. Quarrymen chiseled three deep trenches into the Charanandri ridge, isolating an enormous rectangular monolithic core (82m × 46m × 33m).

Technical Genius: By carving roofs and towers first, the living rock itself acted as scaffolding. Once sculptors finished the uppermost shikhara and finials, they moved down to lower storeys, eliminating both the cost of timber scaffolding and the risk of catastrophic structural settling. No structural stone could ever be added back if mistakenly chiseled away.

Unresolved Question Scholarly Interpretation

The Mystery of the Missing 400,000 Tonnes of Basalt

The Enigma: Mathematical estimates of the excavated rock volume range from 200,000 to over 400,000 metric tonnes of solid Deccan trap basalt. Yet, in the immediate vicinity of Cave 16, there is no colossal mound of quarry debris or slag tailings.

Scholarly Explanations: Archaeologists suggest the basalt chips were not simply discarded. High-density basalt was an invaluable building material: crushed rock was reused for foundations, water reservoirs, dams, defensive ramparts, and paved roads for ancient Elapura, or transported to fortify nearby heights such as Devagiri (later Daulatabad Fort).

Established Evidence Traditional Account

White Plaster & The Snows of Mount Kailash

Cosmic Symbolism: The monument was conceptualized as Mount Kailash—the snow-shrouded Himalayan abode of Lord Shiva. To complete this cosmic metaphor, the dark volcanic basalt was covered with a thick coating of gleaming white lime plaster (chunam).

Historical Memory: As late as the 18th century, European and Mughal travellers recorded the temple under the name Ranga Mahal (Painted Palace) because substantial polychrome frescoes and radiant white stucco still survived on its cornices, vimana tiers, and ceiling vaults.

Traditional Account Established Evidence

The Kokasa Legend vs. Chalukyan Master Guilds

The Folklore: The 15th-century Marathi text Katha-kalpataru by Krishna Yajnavalki recounts that the queen of Elapura took a vow not to break her fast until she saw the shikhara of the Shiva temple. An ingenious master mason named Kokasa arrived and proposed starting from the peak down, allowing the queen to see the pinnacle in just a few days.

Epigraphic Reality: Stylistic analysis reveals profound Dravidian architectural syntax intimately matching the Virupaksha Temple at Pattadakal (built by the Badami Chalukyas). The Rashtrakutas, having vanquished the Chalukyas, commissioned master guild architects (Sarvasiddhi-acharyas) from the south to execute this monumental Dravida program in rock.

Established Evidence Scholarly Interpretation

Multilayered Plaster & Ahilyabai Holkar Restorations

Layer Stratigraphy: Microscopic analysis reveals at least three distinct plaster campaigns. The earliest layer belongs to the 8th-century Rashtrakuta period, showing traces of ochre, cinnabar, and lapis lazuli pigments. A secondary campaign occurred during the Yadava era (12th–13th century).

The Maratha Restoration: In the late 18th century, Maharani Ahilyabai Holkar of Indore sponsored extensive conservation and whitewashing across pilgrimage sites in Western India, applying a fresh lime wash that remains visible in sheltered niches today.

02 · The Ancient Kingdom

A king’s temple on the Deccan

AttributedEstablished

Kailasa is Cave 16 at Ellora, a titanic monolithic shrine to Shiva sculpted by the Rashtrakuta dynasty in the eighth century CE. The primary excavation is historically attributed to King Krishna I (c. 756–774 CE).

Carved directly into the basalt cliffs of the Charanandri range, the temple carries no formal dedicatory inscription. The attribution to Krishna I is corroborated by the Baroda copper plates of 812 CE, though scholars identify multiple architectural phases executed by master guild craftsmen over several decades.

03 · Carved from One Rock

Not built. Uncovered.

EstablishedArchitectural Method

The monument was never constructed with quarried blocks or mortar. Instead, an entire hillside of Deccan trap basalt was excised away from above, isolating an enormous monolith inside a courtyard roughly 82 meters long, 46 meters wide, and 33 meters deep.

Master stonecutters worked exclusively top-down: the finial, shikhara dome, and upper halls were carved first before descending to the foundations, eliminating the need for scaffolding. An estimated 200,000 to 400,000 tonnes of volcanic rock were removed entirely by chisel, mallet, and iron pick.

04 · The Sacred Courtyard

Step through the gateway

EstablishedCosmological Symbol

Passing through the two-storeyed rock-cut gopuram screen, the immense sunlit courtyard opens before you. Colossal 15-meter monolithic victory pillars (dhwaja-stambhas) and life-sized guardian elephants flank the two-storeyed Nandi pavilion.

Surrounding the courtyard, vertical 33-meter cliff faces bear visible chisel grooves from 8th-century quarrymen, with deep, multi-tiered pillared cloisters excavated into the bedrock walls. Overhead, living rock bridges span between the gateway, Nandi mandapa, and main hypostyle hall.

05 · Stories in Stone

The mountain, shaken

Masterpiece

The southern plinth showcases the world-renowned Ravananugraha relief. Below, the twenty-armed demon king Ravana violently shakes Mount Kailasa; above, Shiva serenely steadies the mountain with a touch of his big toe while Parvati clings to him in apprehension and dwarf ganas mock the demon from rock crevices.

Shaivite Iconography

Kailasa houses profound sculptural depictions of Shiva's manifestations: Nataraja in cosmic dance, Tripurantaka drawing his celestial bow, Andhakasuravadhamurti slaying the demon of darkness, Kalarimurti vanquishing time, and the serene Ardhanarishvara.

Monumental Plinth

The entire adhisthana plinth of the temple features an awe-inspiring, continuous frieze of life-sized elephants, lions, and vyalas carved in high relief, appearing to effortlessly hoist the weight of the cosmic mountain upon their shoulders.

06 · Architecture Beyond Imagination

Dravidian genius in living stone

The central vimana rises 32.6 meters in classic Dravidian stepped pyramidal form. Three diminishing tiers (talas) are lined with miniature kutas (square shrines) and shalas (barrel-vault pavilions), culminating in an octagonal dome (shikhara) and water-pot finial (kalasam).

The detached Nandi mandapa rests on a high moulded plinth, sheltering a monolithic recumbent Nandi bull facing the sanctum. Monolithic rock bridges, left intact during excavation, span overhead to connect the gopuram, Nandi hall, and main mandapa porch.

Flanking the central axis stand two monumental 15-meter victory pillars (dhwaja-stambhas), adorned with 16-sided fluting, bulbous bell capitals, and crowned by the sacred Shaivite trident (trishula).

Continuous two-storeyed pillared cloisters run along the north, south, and east cliff perimeters, sheltering over forty colossal mythological relief panels sculpted directly into the mountain rock.

07 · The Passage of Time

Light moves. Stone remains.

Heritage Protection

For over twelve centuries, monsoons, wind, and seismic tremors have weathered the Deccan basalt. The original dazzling white lime plaster and vibrant frescoes have largely yielded to dark volcanic patina and iron oxide tones.

Inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1983, Kailasa remains under the stewardship of the Archaeological Survey of India (ASI)—a triumph of human artistic daring and engineering without equal on Earth.

About Kailasa (Cave 16)

Cave 16 at Ellora (Verul), Chhatrapati Sambhajinagar District, Maharashtra, India. A monolithic rock-cut Shiva temple attributed predominantly to Rashtrakuta King Krishna I (c. 756–774 CE). Inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1983.
